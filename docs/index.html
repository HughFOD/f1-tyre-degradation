How fast do Formula 1 tyres wear out?

Every lap a car gets lighter as fuel burns and slower as its tyres wear. I separated the two for every Grand Prix since 2018, then tested whether wear can be forecast before a race and whether that leads to good pit strategy. Pick a track to see what happened in its latest race.

Separating fuel from tyres

Within a stint, the fuel load falls and the tyres age together, lap after lap, so the two effects can't be told apart. A pit stop breaks the link: the tyres start again from new while the fuel keeps burning. Because drivers stop on different laps, every race has enough of those resets to separate them. The fuel effect comes out at about six hundredths of a second faster per lap, which is what the physics says it should be.

Only laps that show real pace count: no safety cars, no pit laps, no lap 1, and no laps stuck within two seconds of the car in front. That last filter matters most. Without it the soft tyre looks slow, because softs are used at the start, when the field is still queued up. That leaves 165 races and 5,369 practice long runs to learn from.

Can wear be forecast before the race?

For 1,635 stints from 2024 to November 2026, I forecast how much time each would lose using only earlier races and that weekend's practice, then compared with what happened. The error below is for a 20-lap stint.

Modelling wear at all is most of the gain. The red line is the part nobody can forecast: two stints from the same race, on the same compound and at the same tyre ages still differ by that much, from how hard each driver pushed and from traffic. Fitting on the race itself gets close to that line, so only about a third of the remaining error could ever be won back.

Each forecast also comes with a range meant to hold 80% of the time. It held 74% of the time, and 68% on softs, so read them as roughly three-in-four ranges.

Why the pit plans fall short

For each of 61 dry races I took the fastest one or two-stop plan under the pre-race forecast and scored it on how the tyres really wore that day.

75%

of plans start on softs. Only 15% of winners did.

The soft tyre is the weak spot. In clean-air lap times, new softs are barely quicker than hards, so the model sees little reason to avoid them. Teams know softs also grain, overheat and drop off a cliff, which costs track position rather than lap time. Whenever extra data nudged the plans toward what teams actually do, the lap-time model scored the result as slower. A model built on lap times alone can't settle that argument: the next step is to score plans on finishing position.

What I tried, and what held up

Every idea was tuned on 2023 and then tested on 2024 to 2026, races it had never seen.

This weekend's practice long runsKept.Shifting the forecast by how Friday's race simulations went cut the error from 0.975 to 0.950 s, better in every season and on every compound.
One more season of history (2018)Kept.Forecasts about the same; pit plans lost less time (median 9.5 to 7.8 s).
Pirelli's compound choicesDidn't help.Matching each tyre to the same rubber's history at that track was slightly worse, because Pirelli already picks compounds so that soft, medium and hard behave alike at every circuit.
Track temperatureDidn't help.At the same track, hotter races showed no more wear.
Team or driver tyre wearDidn't help.A small gain on 2023 that vanished on 2024 to 2026.
Watching the first part of the raceWorse.Early laps on heavy fuel and a green track mislead about later stints.
Planning for safety cars and uncertain wearDidn't hold up.Looked better on 2023, no better on 2024 to 2026.
Practice data in the pit plansTrade-off.Plans pit when winners do, but the lap-time model calls them slower.
